600T/5000 Heavy-Duty Press Brake in Assembly: How Large Machines Differ from Small Ones

Today's photo: a 600-ton, 5000 mm heavy-duty CNC press brake in assembly — massive red upper beam, reinforced frame and full-length safety guards. "600T/5000" means 600 tons of bending force over a 5-meter bed. A machine like this is a completely different engineering story from a small 40T/1600 unit. Here is what actually changes.

1. What Changes Structurally

  • Frame and weight — a 600T machine carries dozens of tons of steel; the frame is welded from much thicker plates and fully annealed to keep flatness under extreme load. Small machines rely on lighter frames because the forces are modest;
  • Drive system — large machines use bigger hydraulic cylinders (often Y1/Y2 with CNC crowning) to keep the bend line straight across 5 m; small machines can stay simple with a torsion-bar linkage;
  • Tooling — the upper punch and lower die are far larger and heavier, usually segmented so two operators can still handle them;
  • Foundation and handling — a 600T/5000 needs a proper concrete foundation, overhead crane support and often two operators; a small brake just needs a flat floor and one person.

2. What Changes in Capability

Rule of thumb: required tonnage grows with the square of thickness and linearly with length — double the length, double the force; double the thickness, quadruple the force.
  • Small machines (40–160T, up to ~3.2 m) — ideal for thin sheet: enclosures, panels, ducts, kitchen equipment, electrical cabinets. Fast cycles, low energy use, one operator;
  • Heavy machines (300–1000T+, 4–12 m) — the only choice for thick plate and long parts: structural steel, shipbuilding sections, poles and masts, pressure-vessel shells, truck frames. A 600T/5000 can bend plate that a small machine simply cannot move;
  • Precision is not about size — with CNC crowning and a multi-axis back gauge, a large machine can hold the same ±0.1 mm angle repeatability as a small one. Size limits capacity, not accuracy.

3. How to Choose the Right Size

  1. List your thickest material and longest part — these two numbers decide tonnage and bed length;
  2. Add a margin — if your heaviest job needs 450 tons, buy 600 tons: the machine runs unstressed and lasts decades;
  3. Check your workshop — door width, floor strength and crane capacity for heavy machines;
  4. Two machines often beat one — many customers run a small fast brake for thin parts plus a heavy brake for thick ones: total output is higher than forcing everything through one machine.

Not sure which tonnage and length you need? Send us your material thickness, part length and drawings — our engineers will calculate the right machine and quote within 24 hours.

1. What Is a "Non-Standard" Machine?

A standard machine is built to fixed catalog specifications. A non-standard machine is built around your workpiece: tonnage, bending length, frame height, stroke, tooling and control system are all configured for what you actually bend every day. For this customer, 100 tons over 2800 mm with a customized working height and control setup was exactly right for their parts — something no off-the-shelf model could offer.

2. What Can Be Customized on a Press Brake

  • Tonnage and bending length — from small 40T units to heavy-duty tandem lines;

  • Working height and table design — matched to your operators and material flow;

  • CNC controller — the brand and language you prefer;
  • Electrical system — 380V/50Hz, 220V/60Hz or any local standard in your country;

Rule of thumb: if your parts, your workshop or your power supply is special, your machine should be custom-built too.

Heightened CNC Press Brake in Red: Built for Tall-Flange & Deep-Box Parts

Today's update: a heightened CNC press brake in production — grey-black body with a red heightened structure, featuring an enlarged daylight and longer ram stroke for tall-flange workpieces.

1. Why Does a Press Brake Need to Be Heightened?

A standard CNC press brake typically offers an open height (daylight — the working space between the upper and lower tools) of around 300–450 mm, which is enough for most common bending jobs. Problems appear when the workpiece has tall flanges or deep box sections — for example tall cabinet panels, deep U-channels, door frames or large covers. During positioning or turning, the tall bent flange collides with the ram or the top beam, so the part simply cannot be placed or bent.

A heightened press brake solves this with a taller frame, longer ram stroke and enlarged daylight, reserving generous vertical clearance so tall-flange parts can be bent in one setup.

2. How to Tell Whether Your Workpiece Needs a Heightened Machine

The rule of thumb:

Required daylight ≥ Height of the tallest flange + Lower die height + Upper punch height + Safety margin (50–100 mm recommended)

Choose a heightened model if any of the following applies:

  1. Tall bent flanges — the finished upright flange is taller than the daylight of a standard machine;
  2. The part must be lifted or turned over the tooling — especially box-type and deep-cavity parts, which have to be raised above the punch to bend the next side;
  3. The product itself is deep or tall — door frames, elevator decoration panels, cabinet side panels, hoppers, large covers;
  4. Multi-bend parts — every additional bend and turnover increases interference risk; extra height is the safer choice.

💡 Not sure whether your part will collide? Send us the drawing or dimensions — our engineers will calculate the required daylight and recommend the right machine, free of charge.

3. Benefits of a Heightened Press Brake

  1. Tall and deep parts finished in one setup — no outsourcing, no second clamping;
  2. Easier positioning and turning — generous working space lowers the risk of collisions and scratches, protecting surface quality;
  3. More orders accepted — take the deep-box and tall-cabinet jobs that standard machines cannot handle and grow your business;
  4. One machine, dual duty — a heightened model bends standard parts just as well, large or small;
  5. Higher efficiency and yield — less rework and trial bending caused by interference.
