Press Brake Into the Container: How an Export Machine Gets Wrapped, Ramped and Loaded

Today's photo: a press brake mid-load into a 40-foot export container. The machine is fully wrapped in blue protective film and clear stretch wrap, riding on a timber skid, held by two crane slings on red lifting lugs as it is swung off the ramp and into the box. This is the last step before a machine leaves our factory — and the step where a careless packer can undo everything the workshop got right.

1. Why We Containerise Rather Than Flat-Rack

Most of our presses up to about 4000 mm fit a standard container on their side or on a low skid, which means the customer pays ordinary ocean freight instead of open-top or flat-rack rates, and the machine travels sealed against salt air and rain for the whole voyage. The blue film you see is a vapour-corrosion inhibitor layer; the clear wrap over it is mechanical protection against straps, hooks and other cargo shifting in the box.

2. How to Tell a Machine Was Packed Properly

A container is a promise that the machine arrives as it left — the wrap, the skid and the lashing are what keep that promise through 30 days at sea and two port cranes.
  • Dried and oiled before wrapping — ground ways and cylinders are coated and desiccant laid inside so nothing flashes rust in the tropical hold;
  • On a bolted timber skid — the frame is fixed to a skid that the fork trucks and crane slings both key off, so the machine is never slung off its own thin panels;
  • Lift points used, not improvised — the red hooks engage the factory lifting lugs rated for the machine weight, exactly where the load is designed to be taken;
  • Strapped to the container, not the skid — once inside, the whole unit is lashed to the box's twist-locks so it cannot walk during the voyage;
  • Loose tooling in a separate crate — dies, punch segments and the pendant ride in their own marked box, out from under the main load.

3. What This Means for You at the Receiving End

  • One container, one clearance — a standard box is easier and cheaper to clear through customs and to deliver to a site without a special heavy-haul permit;
  • Unpack and go — because the machine ships assembled on its skid, commissioning is a matter of cutting the wrap, levelling and connecting, not re-fitting a frame;
  • Optional add-ons — we can fit side tables, a front support frame or a laser guarding set before the wrap goes on, so accessories travel in the same box instead of a second shipment;
  • Photo record on request — we send the wrapping and lashing photos before the doors close, so you have proof of condition for insurance and for your own receiving check.

130T/4000 Press Brake on the Trailer: How a Four-Metre Machine Leaves the Factory

Today's photos: a 130 T / 4000 mm press brake wrapped in stretch film and lashed to a flatbed trailer, rolling out of our loading bay on its way to the port. This is the most popular mid-size configuration we build — large enough for a serious fabrication shop, still small enough to ship in one piece without splitting the machine apart.

1. What 130T/4000 Actually Covers

130 tonnes — about 1275 kN — spread across a 4000 mm bed. Used along the whole length, that is roughly 4–5 mm mild steel; concentrate the same force on a shorter piece near the centre and the machine handles 8–10 mm. In practice this size serves ventilation ducting, panel and facade work, electrical enclosures, chassis frames and agricultural parts — the middle band of fabrication that neither a bench brake nor a 250-tonne machine covers well.

2. How a Four-Metre Press Brake Gets Packed

A machine that ships in one piece arrives in one piece — every bolt we loosen at the factory is a bolt somebody has to refit, and a datum we hand over to the customer to find again.
  • Loaded as a single unit — the frame, ram and bed travel assembled, sitting on a timber base that spreads the weight across the trailer deck;
  • Stretch film, not a wooden crate — several layers of wrap hold dust and moisture out and let the machine be lifted and re-lashed at the port without unpacking;
  • Lashed, not parked — steel straps from the machine's own lifting lugs down to the trailer's twist-locks take the road shocks of the trip to the terminal;
  • Control pendant protected separately — the screen and its arm are the most fragile parts of any press brake, so they are wrapped and padded on their own, as you can see at the right of the second photo;
  • Loose parts boxed — tooling segments, foot switch, manuals and spare seals travel in a separate marked crate bolted to the same deck.

3. Why Buyers Choose This Size

  • Two independent cylinders, Y1 and Y2 — the labels on the side housings give away the layout: the CNC reads both cylinders and corrects the ram tilt continuously, so parallelism does not depend on the operator;
  • Still one truck, still one container slot area — a 4 m brake needs flat-rack or trailer space, not a special heavy-haul permit the way a 5 or 6 m machine does;
  • Room to grow — shops that start at 100 T tend to fill this machine within two years; buying 130 T now avoids the awkward second purchase;
  • Tooling is standard — 4000 mm machines use common American-European style tooling, so replacement dies are available locally instead of only from us.

Small-Tonnage Press Brakes Ready for Vietnam and Thailand: Compact Machines, Fast Payback

Today's photos: small-tonnage CNC press brakes coming off the final assembly line, destined for Vietnam, Thailand and the wider Southeast Asian market. These are the 30–60 tonne class on 1100–1600 mm beds — the size that sells best into workshops that are moving from outsourced bending to bending in-house.

1. Why Small Tonnage Dominates This Market

Southeast Asian fabricators mostly work thin: 0.5 to 4 mm mild steel and aluminium for ductwork, signage, kitchen equipment, electrical enclosures and brackets. A machine sized for that range costs a fraction of a 130 T brake, fits through a normal workshop door, runs on ordinary shop power, and — the part owners care about — pays for itself in months rather than years. Small does not mean simple: the control in the first photo is the same class of touchscreen we fit to our largest machines.

2. How to Size a Small Press Brake Correctly

Size the machine by the longest bend and the thickest plate you actually buy — not by the biggest job you hope for.
  • Tonnage — up to 4 mm mild steel, 30–40 T is comfortable; 5–6 mm needs 50–60 T; beyond that, move up a class rather than run the press at its limit;
  • V-die opening — the working rule is die opening ≈ 8 × material thickness; too narrow creases the plate, too wide multiplies springback;
  • Bed length — measure the longest sheet you fold, not the longest you cut; a 1500 mm machine with 1250 mm tooling covers most enclosure work;
  • Angle readout — the screen above shows the bending angle (98.60°) and the finished outside dimension (98.50 mm) side by side, so the operator corrects the part in front of him, not the drawing on the wall;
  • Manual handwheel — the big dial under the screen is not a retro detail: it lets one person jog the ram and set the die by hand during tool changes.

3. What to Spend On and What to Skip

  • Worth every dollar — quick clamps — the red-handled clamps in the second photo cut a tool change from twenty minutes to two, which matters most on short-run job work;
  • Worth it — motorised rear gauge — once you run repeat batches, a powered X axis removes the manual measuring step entirely;
  • Optional — segmented upper tooling — short pieces and boxes with closed sides need a punch you can shorten, as shown here;
  • Skip it on a small machine — hydraulic crowning — under about 1600 mm the frame deflection is small enough that mechanical cam compensation does the job for less money.

Our New Fully Enclosed Press Brake: What Full Guarding Actually Buys You

Today's photo: a brand-new model press brake standing in the workshop with its full enclosure fitted — the machine is being finished before it goes up on the product page. White sheet-steel guarding wraps the frame, tinted polycarbonate windows keep the bend line visible, and hinged doors give service access without removing a single panel.

1. Why Enclose the Whole Machine

On a press brake the danger zone is not only the ram — it is the entire working envelope between the upper and lower tool, plus the rear gauge travel behind it. A guard fence at the front stops a hand; an enclosure stops a hand, a sleeve, a dropped off-cut and a curious walk-by. For shops that run more than one shift with rotating operators, that difference is the whole point.

2. How to Judge Safety Guarding From a Photo Like This

Good guarding answers three questions at once: can you see the bend line, can you reach the tooling safely, and does the machine stop the moment you do?
  • Tinted viewing windows — the blue polycarbonate panels let the operator follow the bend continuously without leaning into the working area;
  • Hinged, latched doors — the doors on the side of the hood open the whole tooling zone for setup, so guarding is never "removed for the day" and left off;
  • Ventilation slots — the rows of perforations at the top and bottom of the hood draw heat out of the drive cabinet instead of trapping it around the electronics;
  • Control outside the envelope — the CNC pendant on its articulated arm stands outside the hood, so programming and command happen from a position away from the ram;
  • Machine still accessible — the enclosure is bolted to the frame, not to the floor: it travels with the machine and comes off with hand tools when a component needs service.

3. What Full Guarding Brings to Your Shop

  • Fewer stopping incidents — the most common press brake injury is a hand under a descending ram; physical enclosure removes that path rather than relying on attention;
  • Easier customer and safety audits — buyers who host factory inspections, and insurers who visit yours, read an enclosed machine as a managed risk;
  • Cleaner workshop — the hood keeps scale, oil mist and off-cut fragments inside the machine footprint;
  • Options that fit the frame — light curtains, two-hand control, a powered hold-down and interlocked doors can all be added to this model, so you choose the level of protection your market asks for, not the one we decided for you.

Electric Press Brake in Our Showroom: The Quiet, Clean Machine Behind Our Livestream

Today's photos: the ES-6020 all-electric press brake that lives in our showroom — the very machine we use for livestream demonstrations. Because it runs on servo-electric drives instead of hydraulics, it stays on the showroom floor full-time: no oil leaks, no pump noise, no smell. Customers who visit the factory or join our live stream see the same machine that ships to their workshop.

1. Why an Electric Press Brake Belongs in a Showroom

Hydraulic press brakes need a power unit, hydraulic oil and regular maintenance — they belong on the shop floor, not next to a camera. An all-electric press brake runs on servo motors that drive the ram directly through a ball-screw or belt mechanism. The result is a machine that is quiet enough to film beside, clean enough to keep in a showroom, and precise enough to bend production parts. That is exactly why this ES-6020 doubles as our livestream star and a customer-ready demo unit.

2. How to Tell an Electric Press Brake From a Hydraulic One in Photos

An electric press brake has no oil tank, no pump unit and no hoses — just servo motors on the columns and a ball-screw or belt drive moving the ram.
  • No hydraulic power unit — the white side housings you see contain electric servo drives, not oil reservoirs;
  • Y1/Y2 independent servo columns — each side of the ram is driven by its own servo motor for crowning-free parallelism;
  • Linear-guide backgauge — the close-up above shows hardened rails, servo carriages and a chain drive that position the rear stopper with sub-millimetre repeatability;
  • Clean white-and-red finish — without oil mist or hydraulic leaks, the paint stays showroom-fresh for years.

3. Benefits for Buyers Who Choose Electric

  • Energy savings — servo drives consume power only during the bend stroke, cutting electricity use by 50–70 % compared with a hydraulic pump that runs continuously;
  • Low noise — typical operating noise is 65–70 dB, roughly half of a hydraulic unit, so the machine can sit close to other workstations;
  • Oil-free operation — no hydraulic fluid to replace, filter or dispose of, which matters for clean-room and food-packaging shops;
  • Fast cycle time — electric ram acceleration is near-instant, giving shorter cycle times on thin-gauge work.

Freshly Painted 50T/1600 Press Brake Frames: Whole-Machine Tempering Before the Paint Booth

Today's photos: a row of press brake frames fresh out of the paint booth — a batch of 50T/1600 compact frames produced in volume and now waiting for export shipment. Every one of them went through whole-machine tempering (stress-relief annealing) before a single coat of paint was applied.

1. Why We Temper the Whole Frame Before Painting

Welding locks internal stress into a steel frame; if that stress stays, the frame slowly deforms and the ram loses parallelism. That is why every ZTGAOKE machine — not just selected weldments — is tempered as a complete frame to release welding stress, then milled and only afterwards painted. The result is a frame whose hardness and rigidity are proven before it ever reaches the paint line, not hidden behind it.

2. How to Judge Frame Quality From Photos Like These

Paint decides how a frame looks; tempering decides how long it stays straight — we remove the stress before we apply the colour.
  • Whole-machine tempering, not spot treatment — the complete welded frame is annealed in one cycle, so no zone keeps hidden stress;
  • Thick plate side housings — the upright walls you see here are cut from heavy plate and machined after tempering, keeping the ram guideways true;
  • Uniform industrial coating — an even matte-grey finish seals the steel against rust during long sea freight;
  • Batch consistency — a whole row of identical frames shows standardized jigs and repeatable quality, not one-off fabrication.

3. What This Batch Means for Buyers

  • 50T/1600 in volume production — this compact size is built in batches, so lead times stay short;
  • Export-ready — the batch is scheduled for overseas shipment, packed to export standard;
  • Rigidity that holds accuracy — a tempered frame keeps bend angles stable year after year, which is what really protects your parts quality.

600T/5000 Heavy-Duty Press Brake in Assembly: How Large Machines Differ from Small Ones

Today's photo: a 600-ton, 5000 mm heavy-duty CNC press brake in assembly — massive red upper beam, reinforced frame and full-length safety guards. "600T/5000" means 600 tons of bending force over a 5-meter bed. A machine like this is a completely different engineering story from a small 40T/1600 unit. Here is what actually changes.

1. What Changes Structurally

  • Frame and weight — a 600T machine carries dozens of tons of steel; the frame is welded from much thicker plates and fully annealed to keep flatness under extreme load. Small machines rely on lighter frames because the forces are modest;
  • Drive system — large machines use bigger hydraulic cylinders (often Y1/Y2 with CNC crowning) to keep the bend line straight across 5 m; small machines can stay simple with a torsion-bar linkage;
  • Tooling — the upper punch and lower die are far larger and heavier, usually segmented so two operators can still handle them;
  • Foundation and handling — a 600T/5000 needs a proper concrete foundation, overhead crane support and often two operators; a small brake just needs a flat floor and one person.

2. What Changes in Capability

Rule of thumb: required tonnage grows with the square of thickness and linearly with length — double the length, double the force; double the thickness, quadruple the force.
  • Small machines (40–160T, up to ~3.2 m) — ideal for thin sheet: enclosures, panels, ducts, kitchen equipment, electrical cabinets. Fast cycles, low energy use, one operator;
  • Heavy machines (300–1000T+, 4–12 m) — the only choice for thick plate and long parts: structural steel, shipbuilding sections, poles and masts, pressure-vessel shells, truck frames. A 600T/5000 can bend plate that a small machine simply cannot move;
  • Precision is not about size — with CNC crowning and a multi-axis back gauge, a large machine can hold the same ±0.1 mm angle repeatability as a small one. Size limits capacity, not accuracy.

3. How to Choose the Right Size

  1. List your thickest material and longest part — these two numbers decide tonnage and bed length;
  2. Add a margin — if your heaviest job needs 450 tons, buy 600 tons: the machine runs unstressed and lasts decades;
  3. Check your workshop — door width, floor strength and crane capacity for heavy machines;
  4. Two machines often beat one — many customers run a small fast brake for thin parts plus a heavy brake for thick ones: total output is higher than forcing everything through one machine.

Not sure which tonnage and length you need? Send us your material thickness, part length and drawings — our engineers will calculate the right machine and quote within 24 hours.

Heightened CNC Press Brake in Red: Built for Tall-Flange & Deep-Box Parts

Today's update: a heightened CNC press brake in production — grey-black body with a red heightened structure, featuring an enlarged daylight and longer ram stroke for tall-flange workpieces.

1. Why Does a Press Brake Need to Be Heightened?

A standard CNC press brake typically offers an open height (daylight — the working space between the upper and lower tools) of around 300–450 mm, which is enough for most common bending jobs. Problems appear when the workpiece has tall flanges or deep box sections — for example tall cabinet panels, deep U-channels, door frames or large covers. During positioning or turning, the tall bent flange collides with the ram or the top beam, so the part simply cannot be placed or bent.

A heightened press brake solves this with a taller frame, longer ram stroke and enlarged daylight, reserving generous vertical clearance so tall-flange parts can be bent in one setup.

2. How to Tell Whether Your Workpiece Needs a Heightened Machine

The rule of thumb:

Required daylight ≥ Height of the tallest flange + Lower die height + Upper punch height + Safety margin (50–100 mm recommended)

Choose a heightened model if any of the following applies:

  1. Tall bent flanges — the finished upright flange is taller than the daylight of a standard machine;
  2. The part must be lifted or turned over the tooling — especially box-type and deep-cavity parts, which have to be raised above the punch to bend the next side;
  3. The product itself is deep or tall — door frames, elevator decoration panels, cabinet side panels, hoppers, large covers;
  4. Multi-bend parts — every additional bend and turnover increases interference risk; extra height is the safer choice.

💡 Not sure whether your part will collide? Send us the drawing or dimensions — our engineers will calculate the required daylight and recommend the right machine, free of charge.

3. Benefits of a Heightened Press Brake

  1. Tall and deep parts finished in one setup — no outsourcing, no second clamping;
  2. Easier positioning and turning — generous working space lowers the risk of collisions and scratches, protecting surface quality;
  3. More orders accepted — take the deep-box and tall-cabinet jobs that standard machines cannot handle and grow your business;
  4. One machine, dual duty — a heightened model bends standard parts just as well, large or small;
  5. Higher efficiency and yield — less rework and trial bending caused by interference.
